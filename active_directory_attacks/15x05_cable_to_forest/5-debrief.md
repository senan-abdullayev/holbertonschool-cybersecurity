# Five minutes with the board

**The one sentence:** a stranger who walks into one of our offices, plugs a laptop into a wall socket, and knows nothing about us can reach the protected engineering files we bought Ravensworth to own — in an afternoon, with no username and no password.

We tested exactly that. We began with nothing: no account, no inside knowledge, just a network cable in a Thornwick office. By the end of the day we could open the controlled-technology documents that live on Ravensworth's systems — the files that are the whole reason for that acquisition, and that we are obligated to keep tightly held.

Here is the part that should stay with you. When we bought Ravensworth, we joined their network to ours and quietly treated their systems as someone else's concern. They are not. We showed that whoever takes over the Thornwick network — which today takes very little — also holds the keys to the Ravensworth files. Those files are protected only by the strength of our own front door, and our front door does not hold.

What would it cost if the person in that chair had not been us? These are export-controlled documents. If a competitor or a foreign party walked out with them, the damage is not a repair bill. It is the value of the acquisition itself, our standing with the regulators who govern this technology, and the trust of the customers and partners who assume this material is safe with us. That is a company-level event, not a technical one — and because the intruder needs no password and locks no one out, we might not know it had happened until the harm was already done.

The encouraging part: the single most important repair is small. One maintenance account had been handed far more control than it needed, behind an easily-guessed password. Correcting that one account — about a day of work — shuts the main path we used. Everything else is in the written report, in priority order.

**The one decision we are asking you to make:** fund the security of the two companies' networks as one — protect what we bought to the same standard as what we already were — and treat that spending as protecting the acquisition, not as an IT cost. Every other fix follows from that choice.
